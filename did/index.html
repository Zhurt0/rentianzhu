1. What is difference-in-differences?

Compare the change over time in a treated group with the change over time in an untreated group. The second difference removes whatever both groups share.

In the canonical 2×2 design there are two groups (treated, control) and two periods (before, after). The estimator is

δDiD = ( Ȳtreated, post − Ȳtreated, pre ) − ( Ȳcontrol, post − Ȳcontrol, pre )

which is numerically the coefficient on the interaction in

Yit = α + β·Treati + γ·Postt + δ·(Treati × Postt) + εit

and identifies the average treatment effect on the treated (ATT) under three assumptions:

Parallel trends

Absent treatment, the treated group’s average outcome would have moved in parallel with the control group’s. Untestable for the post period; pre-trends are only suggestive evidence.

No anticipation

Units do not respond to the treatment before it starts, so pre-period outcomes are untreated potential outcomes.

SUTVA / no spillovers

One unit’s treatment does not change another unit’s outcome, and the composition of the groups is stable.

With many units and periods, the 2×2 regression generalises to two-way fixed effects (TWFE) and its dynamic sibling, the event study:

Yit = αi + λt + δ·Dit + εitstatic TWFE
Yit = αi + λt + Σk≠−1 βk·1{t − Gi = k} + εitevent study, Gi = first treated period

3. The story

From a London water pump to the “credibility revolution” and the post-2018 rewrite of the textbook.

  • 1855John Snow compares cholera deaths across London households served by two water companies before and after one moved its intake upstream — DiD logic before the name existed.
  • 1915Obenauer and von der Nienburg study Oregon’s minimum wage for women by comparing affected and unaffected groups before and after, an early use in labour economics.
  • 1978–85Ashenfelter, then Ashenfelter & Card, evaluate training programmes with before/after comparisons against a comparison group, and find the “Ashenfelter dip”.
  • 1990–94Card’s Mariel boatlift study and Card & Krueger’s New Jersey–Pennsylvania minimum-wage study make DiD the signature design of the credibility revolution. Gruber (1994) adds the triple difference.
  • 2004Bertrand, Duflo & Mullainathan show serial correlation makes conventional DiD standard errors far too small; clustering at the treatment level becomes standard.
  • 2005–06Abadie’s semiparametric DiD (conditional parallel trends) and Athey & Imbens’ changes-in-changes extend DiD beyond the linear model.
  • 2018–21The staggered-adoption literature: Goodman-Bacon, de Chaisemartin & D’Haultfœuille, Callaway & Sant’Anna, Sun & Abraham and Borusyak, Jaravel & Spiess show that TWFE can mislead when effects are heterogeneous, and propose replacements.
  • 2022–Attention shifts to the credibility of parallel trends itself (Roth; Rambachan & Roth), continuous and non-absorbing treatments, and hybrids with synthetic control.

4. Why the textbook estimator broke

When units adopt treatment at different dates, the static TWFE coefficient is a weighted average of every possible 2×2 comparison in the data (Goodman-Bacon 2021). Some of those comparisons use already-treated units as controls. If treatment effects grow or differ across cohorts, those “forbidden comparisons” subtract earlier effects from later ones; the weights can be negative, and the TWFE estimate can even have the opposite sign to every underlying effect (de Chaisemartin & D’Haultfœuille 2020). Event-study leads and lags are contaminated in the same way (Sun & Abraham 2021), so flat pre-trends from a TWFE event study are not reliable evidence.

TWFE is still fine when there is a single treatment date, or when effects are homogeneous across cohorts and time. Otherwise, use one of the heterogeneity-robust estimators below. They all share one idea: only compare treated units to not-yet-treated or never-treated units, then aggregate with sensible weights.

5. The versions

Grouped by the problem each one solves. Commands marked built-in ship with Stata 17+; the rest are community-contributed.

A. Classic designs

built-in2×2

Canonical DiD

Two groups, two periods, or a single treatment date.

didregress (y x) (D), group(state) time(year)
xtdidregress (y x) (D), group(state) time(year)
estat ptrends
estat trendplots
estat granger

Card & Krueger (1994); Angrist & Pischke (2009)

Watch the animation
reghdfeTWFE

Two-way fixed effects & event study

The traditional workhorse. Safe with one treatment date or homogeneous effects.

reghdfe y D, absorb(id year) vce(cluster id)
* event study, t = -1 omitted
reghdfe y lead5-lead2 lag0-lag5, ///
    absorb(id year) vce(cluster id)

Correia (2017); Miller (2023); plotting: eventdd (Clarke & Tapia-Schythe 2021), xtevent (Freyaldenhoven et al. 2019)

Watch the animation
built-inDDD

Triple differences

Adds a within-group unaffected subgroup to difference out group-specific shocks. Needs only that the gap in trends is parallel.

didregress (y) (D), group(state agegrp) time(year)
* or by hand
reghdfe y D, absorb(state#year state#agegrp ///
    agegrp#year) vce(cluster state)

Gruber (1994); Olden & Møen (2022); Ortiz-Villavicencio & Sant’Anna (2025)

Watch the animation
drdidcovariates

Conditional parallel trends

Parallel trends holds only after conditioning on covariates. Outcome regression, inverse-probability weighting, or doubly robust combinations.

drdid y x1 x2, ivar(id) time(year) tr(treat) all

Heckman, Ichimura & Todd (1997); Abadie (2005); Sant’Anna & Zhao (2020)

B. Diagnosing TWFE

bacondecomp

Goodman-Bacon decomposition

Splits the TWFE coefficient into all 2×2 comparisons and their weights; shows how much comes from “later vs. already treated”.

xtset id year
bacondecomp y D, ddetail

Goodman-Bacon (2021)

Watch the animation
twowayfeweights

Negative-weights check

Counts how many ATTs receive negative weight in the TWFE regression and how sensitive the estimate is to heterogeneity.

twowayfeweights y id year D, type(feTR)

de Chaisemartin & D’Haultfœuille (2020)

C. Staggered adoption: heterogeneity-robust estimators

csdidbuilt-in: xthdidregress

Callaway & Sant’Anna

Estimates group-time ATT(g,t) for each cohort and period against never- or not-yet-treated units, then aggregates. Handles covariates (doubly robust).

* gvar = first treated year, 0 if never treated
csdid y x, ivar(id) time(year) gvar(g) method(dripw)
estat simple
estat event
csdid_plot
* Stata 18+
xthdidregress aipw (y x) (D), group(id)
estat aggregation, dynamic graph

Callaway & Sant’Anna (2021)

Watch the animation
eventstudyinteract

Sun & Abraham

Interaction-weighted event study: cohort-specific leads and lags, averaged with cohort-share weights. Closest to the familiar regression.

eventstudyinteract y lead* lag*, ///
    cohort(g) control_cohort(never) ///
    absorb(i.id i.year) vce(cluster id)

Sun & Abraham (2021)

Watch the animation
did_imputation

Imputation estimator

Fit unit and time effects on untreated observations only, impute Y(0) for treated ones, average the differences. Efficient under homoskedasticity.

* Ei = first treated year, missing if never
did_imputation y id year Ei, ///
    horizons(0/5) pretrends(4) autosample
event_plot, default_look

Borusyak, Jaravel & Spiess (2024)

Watch the animation
did2s

Two-stage DiD

Same imputation idea in two regressions: residualise on fixed effects from untreated observations, then regress on treatment. GMM standard errors.

did2s y, first_stage(i.id i.year) ///
    second_stage(i.D) treatment(D) cluster(id)

Gardner (2021); Butts & Gardner (2022)

Watch the animation
jwdid

Extended TWFE

Wooldridge’s point: TWFE is not the problem, the restrictive specification is. Saturate with cohort × time interactions; extends naturally to logit and Poisson.

jwdid y, ivar(id) tvar(year) gvar(g)
estat simple
estat event
* nonlinear
jwdid y, ivar(id) tvar(year) gvar(g) method(poisson)

Wooldridge (2021, 2023)

Watch the animation
stackedev

Stacked DiD

Build a clean 2×2 dataset for each adoption event (treated cohort plus clean controls), stack them, and run one regression with stack-specific fixed effects.

stackedev y lead* lag*, cohort(g) time(year) ///
    never_treat(never) unit_fe(id) clust_unit(id)

Cengiz, Dube, Lindner & Zipperer (2019); Wing, Freedman & Hollingsworth (2024)

Watch the animation
lpdid

Local-projections DiD

Horizon-by-horizon long-difference regressions restricted to newly treated and clean control observations. Fast, flexible with covariates and non-absorbing treatment.

lpdid y, unit(id) time(year) treat(D) pre(5) post(10)

Dube, Girardi, Jordà & Taylor (2023)

staggered

Efficient estimator under random timing

If treatment timing is (quasi-)random, pre-period outcomes can be used like covariates in an experiment for a more precise estimate.

staggered y, i(id) t(year) g(g) estimand(simple)

Roth & Sant’Anna (2023b); Athey & Imbens (2022)

D. Treatments that are not binary and absorbing

did_multiplegt_dyn

Switching on and off, any intensity

Compares “switchers” to units with the same baseline treatment that have not switched yet. Works with binary, discrete or continuous, non-absorbing treatments.

did_multiplegt_dyn y id year D, ///
    effects(5) placebo(3) cluster(id)

de Chaisemartin & D’Haultfœuille (2020, 2024)

Watch the animation
did_haddose

Continuous treatment / dose

With a continuous dose, parallel trends alone does not identify a dose-response curve; a stronger assumption is needed. did_had covers designs where everyone gets some dose.

did_had y id year D, effects(3) placebo(2)

Callaway, Goodman-Bacon & Sant’Anna (2024); de Chaisemartin et al. (2024)

Watch the animation
fuzzydid

Fuzzy DiD

Treatment rate rises more in one group than the other, but no group is fully treated or untreated. Wald-DiD, time-corrected Wald, and changes-in-changes Wald.

fuzzydid y G T D, did tc cic

de Chaisemartin & D’Haultfœuille (2018)

cicdistributional

Changes-in-changes

Nonlinear generalisation: identifies the whole counterfactual distribution (quantile effects), invariant to monotone transformations of the outcome.

cic continuous y treat post, at(10(10)90)

Athey & Imbens (2006); Roth & Sant’Anna (2023a)

Watch the animation

E. When parallel trends is doubtful

honestdid

Honest DiD (sensitivity)

Reports confidence sets that stay valid if the post-period violation of parallel trends is at most M times the largest pre-period violation, or bounded in smoothness.

* after an event-study regression
honestdid, pre(1/5) post(6/10) mvec(0(0.5)2)

Rambachan & Roth (2023)

Watch the animation
pretrends

Power of pre-trend tests

Pre-tests have low power and conditioning on passing them distorts inference. Computes the trend your pre-test would detect with given power.

pretrends power 0.5, pre(1/5) post(6/10)

Roth (2022)

Watch the animation
sdidsynth

Synthetic control & synthetic DiD

Re-weight control units (and, in SDID, pre-periods) so the comparison group matches the treated group’s pre-trend, then difference.

sdid y id year D, vce(bootstrap) graph
synth y y(2000) y(2005) x1, trunit(3) trperiod(2006)

Abadie, Diamond & Hainmueller (2010); Arkhangelsky et al. (2021); Clarke et al. (2024)

Watch the animation
fect

Factor models / matrix completion

Replace additive unit and time effects with interactive fixed effects (latent factors) to allow unit-specific responses to common shocks.

fect y, treat(D) unit(id) time(year) ///
    method("ife") r(2) se

Xu (2017); Athey et al. (2021); Liu, Wang & Xu (2024)

F. Inference

vce(cluster)

Cluster at the level of treatment

Outcomes are serially correlated within units, so cluster by the unit at which treatment is assigned (state, firm), not unit-year.

Bertrand, Duflo & Mullainathan (2004); Abadie et al. (2023)

boottest

Few clusters

With fewer than roughly 40 clusters, or very few treated clusters, use the wild cluster bootstrap or randomisation inference.

reghdfe y D, absorb(id year) vce(cluster state)
boottest D, cluster(state) reps(9999)

Cameron, Gelbach & Miller (2008); Conley & Taber (2011); Roodman et al. (2019)

7. A practical workflow

  1. Describe the design. One treatment date or staggered? Absorbing or reversible? Binary or continuous? Are there never-treated units? Plot the rollout (panelview).
  2. Plot raw means by cohort over time before running any regression.
  3. Single date: didregress or reghdfe with an event study is fine.
  4. Staggered: run TWFE as a baseline, diagnose it with bacondecomp / twowayfeweights, then report at least one robust estimator (csdid, did_imputation, eventstudyinteract, jwdid, lpdid). They usually agree; if they do not, find out which comparison drives the gap.
  5. Choose the control group deliberately: never-treated versus not-yet-treated changes the parallel-trends assumption you are making.
  6. Assess parallel trends honestly: show the event study, report honestdid bounds and pretrends power instead of relying on an insignificant pre-test.
  7. Cluster at the level of treatment assignment; use boottest with few clusters.
